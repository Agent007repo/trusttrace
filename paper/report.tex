\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) are entering systems in which several organisations share data and delegate work to AI services. Trustworthy-AI frameworks name the qualities such systems should have, but give architects little help turning the trust expectations of different stakeholders into requirements, decisions and evidence, especially when those expectations conflict. We present TrustTrace, a design-science artefact set for this translation. It comprises (i) a typed trust metamodel that records who trusts whom, to do what, under which condition and with which evidence; (ii) a step-wise elicitation and analysis method; (iii) a synthetic but realistic multi-party logistics compliance case with \NReq{} trust requirements, \NConflicts{} explicit conflicts, \NDecisions{} architecture decision records with structured reflection and \NPatterns{} reusable patterns; (iv) an executable reference implementation whose traceability tool joins each requirement to its decision, component and passing scenario test; and (v) a feasibility pilot, with its protocol frozen in git before collection, of LLM-assisted trust-requirement extraction. \input{generated/abstract_results.tex} The work is a first design cycle. Its validation is analytical and test-based. A practitioner study is designed but not yet run.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Logistics is a natural setting for LLM assistance. A single cross-border shipment involves a shipper, a freight forwarder, a carrier, a customs broker, a customs authority and, increasingly, platform and model providers. LLMs can suggest tariff classifications, flag inconsistencies between an invoice and a packing list, and answer regulatory questions. Each of these tasks changes who relies on whom. A broker who accepts a suggested classification relies on the assistant, on the passages it retrieved and on the platform operator that configured it. A shipper relies on the platform not to pass its prices to the carrier. An auditor relies on records that nobody could have silently changed.

Trust in this sense is relational and conditional. Organisational research defines it as a willingness to be vulnerable to another party based on expectations about that party \cite{mayer1995}. Work on automation stresses that the goal is appropriate reliance, not maximal trust \cite{lee2004}. Jacovi et al.\ formalise trust in AI as a trustor's belief that a model will uphold an anticipated contract \cite{jacovi2021}. These views share a structure that architects can use: a trustor, a trustee, an entrusted scope, and conditions under which reliance is warranted.

Existing guidance mostly lists qualities. The European Commission's High-Level Expert Group names seven requirements for trustworthy AI, including human agency and oversight, transparency, privacy and data governance, and accountability \cite{hleg2019}. Requirements engineering for AI is an active field \cite{ahmad2023,vogelsang2019,habibullah2024}, and recent work points out what is still missing for responsible AI \cite{zowghi2023}. Pattern catalogues collect good practice \cite{lu2024}. What remains hard is the step from \emph{these qualities matter} to \emph{this requirement, decided this way, verified by this test}, in an ecosystem where qualities conflict. More transparency can expose confidential data. More automation can reduce human control. Easier provider replacement, which supports sovereignty, can break validated behaviour.

This report asks two research questions, stated in the repository's preregistration:
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{0pt}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Can a typed trust chain expose missing source, decision, component and passing-scenario evidence in an authored logistics case?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Does metamodel-guided prompting improve LLM extraction of atomic trust requirements over a naive prompt?
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Contributions.} (1) A trust metamodel and analysis method (Section~\ref{sec:method}). (2) A multi-party logistics case with \NReq{} requirements, \NConflicts{} conflicts, \NDecisions{} decision records and \NPatterns{} patterns (Section~\ref{sec:case}). (3) An executable reference implementation and traceability gate (Section~\ref{sec:impl}). (4) A feasibility pilot of LLM-assisted extraction, with an automatic matcher checked against a blinded human audit (Section~\ref{sec:b10}). (5) A protocol for the practitioner study that the artefacts still need (Section~\ref{sec:future}).

\section{Background and related work}
\textbf{Trust and reliance.} Mayer et al.\ separate trust from the trustworthiness factors that justify it \cite{mayer1995}. Lee and See argue that designs should support calibrated reliance \cite{lee2004}. Jacovi et al.\ distinguish warranted from unwarranted trust in AI \cite{jacovi2021}, and Toreini et al.\ relate trust qualities to concrete machine-learning techniques \cite{toreini2020}. We borrow the trustor, trustee and condition structure from this line of work and make it a data model.

\textbf{Traceability and assurance.} Requirements traceability has long been framed as the ability to follow a requirement forwards and backwards through its life \cite{gotel1994,cleland2014}. Assurance cases argue explicitly why a system meets a claim, and AMLAS adapts that argument to machine-learning components \cite{hawkins2021}. TrustTrace is lighter than a full assurance case: it checks that links exist and that the linked test passed, not that the argument is sound. Reference architectures for foundation-model agents \cite{lu2023ra} describe the components our decisions place trust boundaries around.

\textbf{Requirements engineering for AI and LLMs.} Mapping studies show that requirements work for AI concentrates on data and model qualities, with less attention to multi-stakeholder concerns \cite{ahmad2023}. Practitioners report difficulty specifying ML quality \cite{vogelsang2019}, and the scope of non-functional requirements for ML is still being worked out \cite{habibullah2024}. LLMs are now studied as assistants for requirements work itself \cite{ronanki2023,arora2024}. Extracting obligations from regulatory text has a longer history, from manual rule analysis \cite{breaux2008} to NLP pipelines for legal metadata \cite{sleimi2018}; our pilot is a narrow, reproducible test of whether small local LLMs can do part of this on short stakeholder statements, scored with an embedding-based matcher in the spirit of BERTScore \cite{zhang2020}. Goal-oriented approaches such as the NFR framework \cite{chung2000} and iStar 2.0 \cite{dalpiaz2016} model how design choices help or hurt softgoals. We use a lightweight softgoal graph in that spirit.

\textbf{Architecture decisions and their human side.} Architecture decisions are made by people under uncertainty and bias \cite{tang2017}. Studying them needs social-science methods as well as engineering ones \cite{razavian2019}. Reflection influences how designers reason \cite{razavian2016}, and serious games such as the Ethics-Aware DecidArch game prompt teams to consider stakeholder values \cite{alidoosti2023}. Razavian et al.\ envision architectural knowledge systems that act as on-demand decision companions \cite{razavian2023}. Our decision records add a reflection section motivated by this work. Quality attribute scenarios and ATAM-style analysis \cite{kazman2000} supply the six-part scenario form we use for tests.

\textbf{Engineering AI-enabled systems.} AI components behave differently from conventional ones \cite{ozkaya2020}. Test-gated readiness for ML \cite{breck2017}, retrieval-augmented generation \cite{lewis2020}, indirect prompt injection \cite{greshake2023}, contestability \cite{alfrink2022}, transparency logs \cite{rfc6962}, scoped authorisation \cite{rfc6749} and sovereignty as an architectural objective \cite{esposito2026} each inform one of our patterns.

\textbf{Regulatory context.} The case touches the AI Act \cite{aiact}, as amended in 2026 \cite{omnibus2026}, the GDPR \cite{gdpr}, the Union Customs Code \cite{ucc} and the electronic freight transport information (eFTI) regulation \cite{efti}. We cite these at the level of the act. Article-level text has not yet been verified for this case, and the case makes no legal classification.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
We follow design science research \cite{hevner2004,wieringa2014}: problem investigation, treatment design and treatment validation. This report covers one cycle.

\textbf{Metamodel.} The metamodel is a typed Pydantic schema with \NEntityTypes{} entity types. A \emph{concern} records provenance (synthetic, documentary or interview). A \emph{trust requirement} names its source concern, trustor, trustee, entrusted scope, condition, fit criterion, priority, and its links to risks, conflicts, tactics, decisions, components and tests. A \emph{quality attribute scenario} gives the six SEI parts: source, stimulus, artefact, environment, response and response measure. \emph{Conflicts} name the requirements in tension and the decision that resolves them. \emph{Evidence} records its provenance (synthetic, measured, assumed, pending). Every link is checked: a dangling identifier, an unresolved conflict or a missing decision document fails validation.

\textbf{Analysis method.} An architect (1) maps stakeholders and trust relationships; (2) elicits concerns from documents, interviews or workshops, keeping provenance; (3) refines each concern into requirements whose fit criterion can fail; (4) identifies conflicts between requirements; (5) writes six-part scenarios; (6) records decisions with considered options, consequences and a reflection on assumptions, dissenting stakeholders and possible bias; and (7) traces each requirement to an executed test. A requirement counts as covered only when a decision, a component, a scenario and a \emph{passing} requirement-tagged test all exist. Coverage is computed from the JUnit report of an actual test run, never from a declared flag.

\section{Reference implementation and traceability}\label{sec:impl}
The implementation is a Python library with a small FastAPI demo. Components are a versioned sharing policy with disclosure receipts, a purpose-bound delegation registry, retrieval with citation checking, a model gateway (egress gate, strict output contract, evaluation-gated replacement), a review queue, contest and override records, explanation cards that separate verbatim quotes from generated interpretation, unit-aware consistency checks, a retention store, and a hash-chained audit log checkpointed to a separate anchor store. Everything is in-process and synthetic. There is no authentication, no real tenant and no customs submission.

Each requirement links to one or more scenario tests marked with its identifier. For example, the test for \req{TR-034} retrieves a passage that instructs the system to approve an item and checks that the item stays pending and that an output carrying a tool call is rejected. The test for \req{TR-033} rewrites the whole audit log and checks that verification fails against the stored anchor.

\textbf{RQ1 result.} \input{generated/trace_results.tex}

The answer to RQ1 is a qualified yes, and it is a demonstration of mechanism rather than an evaluation. The gate checks links that the same authors wrote, so its value is that a missing link fails loudly: it lists the two requirements that still need verified legal sources, and during development it flagged every should requirement before its test existed. A seeded-defect study, in which links are removed or broken at random and detection is measured, would turn this into an evaluation. It cannot tell whether a requirement is the right one, whether a scenario is realistic, or whether a stakeholder would accept the trade-off. Those questions need people.

\section{B10 pilot: LLM-assisted trust requirement extraction}\label{sec:b10}
\textbf{Design.} The full study, specified in a preregistration committed to the repository on 13 October 2026, plans 24 texts, two model families, five seeds, three prompt conditions and blinded human matching of every prediction. Two of its inputs (verified legal excerpts and an independent retrieval corpus) were not ready, so we ran a reduced feasibility pilot under a dated appendix frozen before any output existed. Ten of the twelve synthetic stakeholder texts were drawn with a fixed seed. Each is a one- or two-sentence user story of 14 to 21 words, written by the same project that wrote the guided prompt. The author labelled every atomic obligation in them, with trustor, trustee, scope, condition, fit criterion and an exact evidence span, using a labelling tool that never showed model output. Two local open-weight models, Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \cite{qwen25} and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 \cite{mistral7b}, both 4-bit quantised, ran under two conditions (Qwen on a 2-thread cloud CPU; Mistral on an Apple M1 Max GPU after the cloud run was interrupted, a dated deviation): a naive instruction and a metamodel-guided one. Each text, model and condition was sampled with three seeds, giving 120 generations. A pre-collection check showed that the Mistral chat template silently drops a separate system message, so both models received the instructions inside the user turn.

\textbf{Matching.} Predictions were matched to gold labels automatically by a rule fixed before collection: pair score $=0.6\cdot\cos(\mathrm{statements}) + 0.4\cdot F_1(\mathrm{evidence\ tokens})$, with BGE-small embeddings \cite{bge}, a 0.6 threshold and a maximum-weight one-to-one assignment. To test whether the rule agrees with human judgement, \input{generated/audit_method.tex} We report Cohen's $\kappa$ \cite{cohen1960} and treat F1 as unreliable if $\kappa<0.6$. The guided minus naive difference is paired by text and summarised with a 2000-resample percentile bootstrap over texts \cite{efron1979}.

\section{Discussion}
\textbf{What the artefacts make possible.} The chain turns a vague claim, such as ``the platform protects shippers'', into a set of checkable statements: which field may reach which role (P1), for which purpose and until when (P5), with what receipt (P6), and what an affected party can do when it goes wrong (P7). The conflict matrix makes the price of each choice explicit. The reflection sections in the decision records are meant to make it harder to treat a decision as obvious; whether they do is a question for the practitioner study. For a research programme that studies how stakeholders reason about openness, automation, oversight and accountability, these artefacts are instruments: something to put in front of practitioners and observe how they use, contest or ignore them.

\textbf{What the pilot suggests.} The pilot was designed to answer feasibility questions, and it answers most of them. The labelling scheme could be applied to all ten texts; the tool recorded only a few minutes of entry time, and drafting time was not measured. Small local models produce schema-valid output at the rates in Table~\ref{tab:b10}. \input{generated/audit_discussion.tex}The guided versus naive contrasts are small and rest on very few texts: most texts tie, because each has only one or two obligations, so per-run F1 takes few distinct values. The Mistral difference sits at the size the full study's hypothesis treats as meaningful, which is exactly why it needs more texts, not a claim now. Guidance also lowered exact-evidence rates, a grounding cost the full study should measure directly.

\section{Threats to validity}
\textbf{Construct.} All concerns are authored. They may encode the author's view of logistics rather than practitioners'. The metamodel's slots may not capture how people actually talk about trust. Requirement F1 measures agreement with one person's labels, not correctness. The source texts and the guided prompt were written by the same project, which may favour the guided arm.

\textbf{Internal.} Scenario tests verify behaviour of an in-process prototype under the tested conditions only. The matcher's weights and threshold were assumed. The two models ran on different backends. \input{generated/backend_check.tex} Blinding of the audit is procedural: the matcher's decisions are recomputable from the repository. The Mistral model file is a third-party conversion whose source revision we did not verify. Both conditions share the output contract, so the contrast isolates the guidance text only.

\textbf{External.} One synthetic case in one domain. Ten one- or two-sentence user stories. Two small, quantised local models. Legal text is absent from the pilot, and legal provisions were cited only at the level of the act.

\textbf{Conclusion.} Ten text clusters give wide intervals. We make no significance claims and do not test H1 confirmatorily. There is a single annotator and no inter-annotator agreement.

\section{Future work}\label{sec:future}
The next design cycle is empirical. A practitioner study is fully specified in the repository: semi-structured interviews with broker, forwarder, compliance and architecture roles; a workshop in which participants use conflict cards derived from Table~\ref{tab:conflicts} to make and justify decisions, inspired by serious-game work on architectural decisions \cite{alidoosti2023}; consent and data-management templates; and a thematic analysis plan. Its questions follow directly from this report. Do practitioners recognise the trustor and trustee slots in their own reasoning? Which conflicts do they see that we missed? Does the reflection section change their decisions or only their documentation? Do disclosure receipts and contest channels matter to SMEs, or only to auditors?

The full B10 study adds verified legal excerpts, a retrieval arm, a second annotator and five seeds, sized from the pilot's observed variance. A four-year agenda would move from this synthetic case to observed architectural decision-making in an LLM-for-logistics consortium, toward a reference architecture whose patterns have been tried, contested and revised with the people who must rely on them.

\section*{Reproducibility}
Code, data, configuration, raw model outputs, labels and analysis scripts are in the repository, and the audit decisions are added once the audit is complete. \texttt{make test} runs the quality gates and scenario tests. \texttt{make analyze} regenerates the pilot and trace results, the case counts and the conflict table from the committed files; the pattern table is a hand summary of \texttt{docs/patterns.md}. The pilot protocol, deviations and model checksums are committed, and the pilot protocol was committed before collection.
